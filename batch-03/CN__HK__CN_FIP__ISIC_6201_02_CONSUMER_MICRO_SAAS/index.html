None. Hong Kong limited partnerships have no statutory minimum paid-up capital. E-government filing portal

Export 0% zero-rating conditions

A Chinese FIP supplying cloud software and SaaS services to overseas consumers or to a foreign merchant-of-record such as Paddle or Lemon Squeezy may claim the 0% VAT export rate only where the service is a qualifying cross-border software or information technology service, the service is wholly consumed outside the PRC, the recipient is outside China, payment is received in foreign currency through legitimate cross-border payment channels, and the FIP has completed the STA cross-border zero-rate/exemption registration filing. The FIP must be a general VAT taxpayer to claim the 0% rate with input VAT credit preservation; a small-scale taxpayer may instead claim exemption but cannot claim input credits. The zero-rating claim is evidenced by substantive offshore-consumption documentation rather than physical customs clearance because pure SaaS services are intangible.

  • Customs Export Declaration (Export Customs Declaration for Goods) - mandatory only where goods are physically exported; not applicable to pure SaaS services
  • Bill of Lading or Master/House Air Waybill - mandatory only for physical exports; not applicable to pure SaaS services
  • Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or SAFE-compliant Bank Foreign Exchange Collection Receipt evidencing export proceeds repatriation into the PRC
  • Signed service export contract, SaaS subscription agreement, or merchant-of-record agreement with the overseas party
  • Fully Digitalized Electronic Invoice (FDEI) issued by the FIP for the exported service
  • STA cross-border service zero-rate or exemption registration filing acknowledgment
  • Digital evidence of offshore consumption, including customer IP geolocation, subscription records, connection logs, and overseas user identity details

China operates the STA Fully Digitalized Electronic Invoice (FDEI) platform. Taxpayer invoices must be issued through the STA FDEI system or the legacy VAT invoice management system, with invoice data transmitted to the STA in real time. The FDEI carries a unique tax invoice number and an official anti-fraud digital stamp generated by the STA on a one-invoice-one-number basis; no physical tax-control device is required for FDEI issuance. General VAT taxpayers must issue Special VAT Invoices or ordinary FDEIs through the STA platform, and accounting or e-commerce billing systems may integrate with the FDEI platform via STA-certified APIs. No third-party invoice document is tax-recognized without STA validation and filing.

The STA may audit export zero-rating claims and recover all underpaid VAT, deny input VAT credits, and impose daily late-payment interest of 0.05% plus an administrative penalty of between 50% and 500% of the unpaid tax. Fraudulent or unverifiable export zero-rating claims trigger retrospective clawback of the VAT benefit and may constitute the crime of fraudulently obtaining export tax rebates under Article 204 of the Criminal Law of the PRC, punishable with fines, criminal detention, or imprisonment up to life imprisonment for especially large amounts. Taxpayers may also have FDEI issuance rights suspended, be removed from the cross-border zero-rate registration list, and be subject to enhanced audit scrutiny for all other tax periods.

Banking & Treasury Architecture

Central-bank FX diagnosis, tier-1 local banks vs offshore digital EMI matrix, and KYC rejection defense guide.

Foreign Exchange Regime & Central Bank Policy

Current-account convertibility

Mainland China operates a managed, partially convertible foreign-exchange system with PBOC/SAFE oversight. Current-account transactions, including software subscription receipts, service fees, royalties, and bona fide profit transfers, are convertible subject to authenticity documentation and bank verification. Capital-account flows, such as inbound equity contributions, cross-border debt, and outbound investment from the Chinese FIP, are restricted and require prior registration or approval through the SAFE Capital Information System. For this FIP, incoming USD from the Hong Kong partner is treated as foreign direct investment and must be registered before deposit into a designated foreign-exchange capital account. Conversion of the capital injection into CNY is permissible only for the negative-list-exempt operating purposes of the micro-SaaS business; unlawful reuse, intercompany lending, or property investment is prohibited. Profit remittances to non-resident partners are allowed under the current-account regime but only after audited financial statements, tax clearance, and partnership distribution resolutions are filed. The entire operational environment can therefore be characterized as current-account flexible but capital-account controlled, with explicit documentary chain requirements, real-time transaction monitoring, and consequential compliance friction for any cross-border treasury activity.

Export proceeds surrender rules

Under the 2012 SAFE reform, mandatory conversion and surrender ratios were abolished; the FIP may retain 100% of export foreign currency receipts in a designated current account or convert them to CNY at its discretion. The remaining statutory discipline is repatriation and reporting: proceeds must be remitted to the FIP's China-banked designated current account within the contracted payment timeline, and the receipts must be validated by the bank against the SAFE Goods Trade Monitoring System. Payment terms in excess of 90 days require the FIP to file a delayed-receipt trade-credit report through the SAFE system, usually within 30 days of the underlying export; failure to maintain matching declarations can lead to account restrictions and the blocking of unallocated receipts. No fixed statutory conversion ratio applies.

Central bank supervision: The People's Bank of China (PBOC) and the State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE), with the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) as the authoritative regulator for the CNH and offshore banking leg

Offshore Digital Bank / EMI Whitelist

Cross-border EMI / Fintech rails Fit: High

Low for onshore treasury clearing. A Chinese-domiciled FIP is required to route domestic FX receipts, payments, and capital-account flows through PBOC-licensed banking institutions. Offshore digital EMIs and Paddle/Lemon Squeezy pay-in platforms can function only as foreign merchant-of-record collection channels; they cannot replace the FIP's onshore bank account, cannot execute payment-for-settlement without a Chinese bank, and are not licensed to provide banking or SAFE foreign-exchange services in China. Using them as a settlement rail without a corresponding onshore account would constitute unlicensed cross-border payment activity. A narrow valid use is as an offshore receivable collection and PSP layer, followed by periodic repatriation to the onshore bank via compliant trade/SWIFT flows; however an EMI cannot perform the capital conversion or custody duties.

Cross-Border Clearing Corridors & Settlement Fit

Compliant routing for the capital-side begins with the Hong Kong partner remitting USD through SWIFT MT103 to the FIP's designated Foreign Exchange Capital Account at Bank of China, with the payment narrative carrying the SAFE FDI registration reference and Balance-of-Payments code 622011 (investment capital). The receiving bank verifies the funds against the SAFE Capital Information System, enters the deposit in the account, and the FIP then completes the payment-for-settlement statement if conversion to CNY is required, declaring that the RMB will be used only for the micro-SaaS operations and not for prohibited negative-list outflows. A PRC CPA performs capital verification through the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System, confirming that the received amount matches the contribution schedule. No capital can be repatriated to the HK partner until the FIP's profit distribution process has been passed and tax clearance certificate issued.For operational trade settlements from the Chinese FIP: first, domestic vendor payments are made in CNY via CIPS or RTGS from the operational account. Second, payments to offshore cloud, marketing, or software vendors use SWIFT MT103 routed through the FIP's FX current account, attaching the service contract, commercial invoice, and SAFE service-trade transaction code such as 223010 for computer services. Third, for any eventual physical-goods procurement where the SaaS business may later sell hardware, a Commercial Letter of Credit through a Chinese issuing bank or Documentary Collection through a correspondent bank is available, though the software-subscription model generally relies on MT103 for supplier disbursements. Fourth, partner profit repatriation to Hong Kong requires an Investment Income and Profit Distribution remittance under code 621011, supported by audited financials, tax clearance, and the partnership distribution resolution.Onshore RMB/FX forward contracts are permitted for current-account receivables and payables under SAFE real-demand rules, provided the FIP can show genuine trade exposure, a valid underlying invoice or contract, and no rollover without a new trade event.Onshore FX swaps and currency swaps are available through licensed Chinese banks for hedging CNY/USD working-capital exposure associated with cross-border receipts, subject to SAFE's documentary review and a ban on speculative or leveraged positions.Offshore CNH/USD non-deliverable forwards can be used at the Hong Kong treasury layer for CNH-exposure hedging, but onshore NDFs are prohibited for the China-registered FIP; options and synthetic forwards may be permitted only if packaged as structured forward products and fully aligned with real trade flows.

E-commerce / gateway settlement fit: Low for onshore treasury clearing. A Chinese-domiciled FIP is required to route domestic FX receipts, payments, and capital-account flows through PBOC-licensed banking institutions. Offshore digital EMIs and Paddle/Lemon Squeezy pay-in platforms can function only as foreign merchant-of-record collection channels; they cannot replace the FIP's onshore bank account, cannot execute payment-for-settlement without a Chinese bank, and are not licensed to provide banking or SAFE foreign-exchange services in China. Using them as a settlement rail without a corresponding onshore account would constitute unlicensed cross-border payment activity. A narrow valid use is as an offshore receivable collection and PSP layer, followed by periodic repatriation to the onshore bank via compliant trade/SWIFT flows; however an EMI cannot perform the capital conversion or custody duties.

Transfer Pricing & Economic Substance

Master / Local File threshold: HKD 11,000,000 (for controlled transactions in non-tangible goods/services, intangible property, or any transaction not being tangible goods or financial, the Local File is required if the aggregate amount exceeds HKD 11 million; for tangible goods HKD 500 million; for financial transactions HKD 50 million). The Master File is required if the multinational group's revenue exceeds HKD 7.5 billion; CbC Reporting threshold is group revenue of HKD 15 billion. For a micro-SaaS FIP, royalty payments, management fees, software licensing to associated enterprises, or payments to a Mainland parent may trigger the HKD 11 million de minimis threshold.

Economic substance scope: Hong Kong applies the territorial source principle and the FSIE regime. An entity claiming foreign-source income exemption for passive income must demonstrate, through its own staff and premises in Hong Kong, that its core income-generating activities (CIGA) are performed in Hong Kong. This includes making strategic commercial decisions, managing risks, and taking major business decisions. The Inland Revenue Ordinance section 15(1)(d) also deems certain income from Hong Kong as sourced in Hong Kong. Although the term 'Economic Substance Regulations' (ESR) is specific to Caribbean offshore jurisdictions, Hong Kong applies a similar substance requirement under DIPN 62 and the FSIE rules: the taxpayer must have adequate people, premises, and business activities in Hong Kong for the income-bearing activity. A micro-SaaS must show that its contract negotiation, pricing, support, and decision-making are performed by Hong Kong staff, not merely that its server is located offshore.

Fatal Compliance Red Lines & Director Liability

Tax Evasion / Willful Misdeclaration of Hong Kong Profits Tax

Statutory sanction: Under IRO section 80(2), knowingly and wilfully making an incorrect return is punishable by a fine of HKD 10,000 and additionally treble the amount of tax undercharged; imprisonment may also be imposed in serious cases under the IRO and the Theft Ordinance for false accounting. Section 82A allows additional tax of up to 100% of the tax undercharged.

Director personal liability: Directors of the FIP's corporate general partner can be personally assessable and liable if they sign or produce false returns, or through knowing involvement in the fraud. The IRD may issue a departure prevention order / travel restriction under IRO section 76 blocking the director from leaving Hong Kong, apply for a restraint order over personal assets, and pursue criminal prosecution resulting in imprisonment of up to 14 years, plus personal restitution of evaded taxes and treble penalties.
Transfer Pricing Documentation and Arm's Length Violations

Statutory sanction: Under IRO section 61A/61B or section 20, the IRD may disregard the transactions and compute assessable profits as if the arm's length principle had been applied. Type 1 penalties of up to 100% additional tax may be imposed. Failure to maintain contemporaneous TP documentation is subject to a penalty of up to HKD 50,000 and daily default penalty of HKD 500 per day. Documentation may be required under section 51C, for which failure leads to prosecution with fine of HKD 100,000 and six months imprisonment.

Director personal liability: Directors who knowingly cause the FIP to underreport income through artificial transfer prices may be personally liable for the additional tax as controlling officers under IRO. They may be subject to civil fraud claims by the IRD and insolvency administrator (for fraudulent trading). Criminal prosecution under the Theft Ordinance can lead to up to 14 years imprisonment. Travel restrictions and asset freezes may also be imposed during the investigation.
Shell Company / Economic Substance Abuse and DTA / PRC Anti-avoidance

Statutory sanction: Under Hong Kong IRO, the IRD may assess the FIP based on substance and impose penalties under section 82A. Mainland China may invoke the General Anti-Avoidance Rule (GAAR) and the substance-over-form doctrine under the Corporate Income Tax Law, re-characterising the FIP as a tax conduit or permanent establishment (PE) of the Chinese enterprise. PRC tax authorities may impose 10% withholding on deemed China-sourced service or royalty income, plus interest and late payment surcharges. The DTA action may deny treaty benefits under the Limitation on Benefits provisions (if any) or under the principal purpose test for Chinese tax treaties (MLI). The FIP may be deemed to have a PE in Mainland China, resulting in 25% CIT on profits attributable to the PE.

Director personal liability: Directors and shadow founders who operate the micro-SaaS from Mainland China while directing global income through the Hong Kong FIP can be prosecuted for tax evasion in PRC under Article 201 of the Criminal Law for false tax declarations, facing imprisonment up to seven years and a fine of up to five times the tax evaded. In Hong Kong, personal liability may arise through a constructive trust or fraudulent trading; civil liability for abetting a shell scheme; the Inland Revenue Department may hold the promoter/director jointly liable. A travel ban and blacklisting by the PRC tax authorities can be imposed, and in Hong Kong the IRD may prevent departure. Bankruptcy or disqualification as a director is a likely consequence.
